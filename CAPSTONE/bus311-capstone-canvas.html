FactSet activation and Excel setup

Choose one decision path

Growth or efficiency investment

Should the company fund a specific capacity, technology, or process improvement?

Method: Incremental after-tax cash flow and NPV versus doing nothing.

Inputs and scope

Initial equipment and setup cash cost; annual additional sales or cost savings; operating costs; tax; additional working capital; end-of-project recovery. Use a bounded five-year project. Explain any estimated project cost using a public benchmark.

Choose a project with a defensible public cost benchmark. A whole-company acquisition, buyback, debt restructuring, or full enterprise valuation is outside this project.

Working-capital improvement

Should the company reduce cash tied up in inventory or receivables without damaging service or sales?

Method: Incremental cash released, implementation and operating costs, and NPV versus current practice.

Inputs and scope

For receivables, annual sales / 365 × days reduced; for inventory, annual cost of goods sold / 365 × days reduced. Use one lever. Count the release once, explain the duration, and include restoration if the improvement ends.

Use one cash-conversion lever, with two service or revenue risks. Do not count the same cash release again as operating revenue or recurring savings.

Both paths use the Model tab. Compare incremental cash flows with the status quo, whose incremental NPV is zero. The template provides the calculations; you supply and explain the assumptions.

Your six-slide decision briefing

  1. What should the company do? State the company, your name, the proposed action, and one clear recommendation. One sentence naming the decision and its scope.
  2. Why this decision? Explain the business need and how the company makes money. A simple three-year sales chart and two drivers.
  3. What evidence supports it? Show the decisive evidence and the assumption it supports. One peer comparison, one forecast claim, and source IDs.
  4. What do the numbers show? Compare doing nothing with the change in base, upside, and downside cases. Three scenario NPVs, one sensitivity, and the model version/cells.
  5. What could go wrong? Explain two risks, the strongest challenge, and what you changed. One observable condition that would change your recommendation.
  6. What happens next? Name the first action, responsible role, timing, and one measure to monitor. A practical next step and a stop, review, or expand trigger.

Use six core slides plus an optional supporting appendix. Present for up to seven minutes, then answer questions for up to three minutes. The template notes offer a suggested timing plan.

Annotated example: what sufficient work looks like

Harbor Supply Co. (fictional)

All company names, historical figures, and assumptions in this example are fictional teaching data. They are not a student company solution.

Install order-handling equipment for a five-year project.

If the new equipment reduces handling time without raising errors, Harbor can save $3 million annually for five years. Reject this assumption if the pilot savings fall below the break-even level.

  • Three years and two drivers establish enough context to explain the decision.
  • The $8 million cost and annual savings are labeled assumptions, not reported company facts.
  • Annual depreciation is $8 million / 5 years; taxable operating savings are reduced by depreciation.
  • The model compares incremental cash flow with doing nothing. It discounts cash received later.
  • The downside and break-even test reveal what evidence would change the recommendation.

Open the Example tab in the continuing workbook to see the calculations and a justified recommendation. The example is teaching support, not an additional submission.
