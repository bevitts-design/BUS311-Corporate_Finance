Pre-class briefing

Why this matters

Statements organize the evidence; ratios make patterns and comparisons visible. In class, use the starter’s Class Practice tab. After class, compare the supplied fictional retailers TechStyle Retail and Urban Goods. The goal is not merely to reproduce a formula; it is to explain what the result means for a financial decision and what evidence could change the conclusion.

Learning objectives

  • Calculate ratios from consistent inputs
  • Interpret ratios through drivers and peers
  • Avoid denominator and period mismatches

Ratio families

Liquidity

Current and quick ratios provide a snapshot, but analysts also inspect cash conversion and credit access. Too much liquidity may indicate idle capital rather than strength.

Profitability

Margins isolate different layers of economics. ROA and ROE combine income with the asset or equity base used to generate it.

Leverage

Debt ratios and interest coverage capture different dimensions of financing risk. A level that is manageable for stable utilities may be dangerous for cyclical firms.

Interpret, do not merely calculate

DuPont logic

Breaking ROE into components prevents a misleading conclusion. A high ROE driven by better operations is different from a high ROE created by a thin equity base.

Peer context

Compare the same definition, period, and accounting basis. Industry structure, strategy, seasonality, and business mix explain why healthy ratios differ across firms.

Analytical safeguards

Match periods

Income covers a period while balance-sheet values represent dates. Average assets or average equity better align the denominator with the activity that generated the numerator.

Check signs and units

Many spreadsheet errors are unit errors. Label currency scale, express percentages as decimals in formulas, and verify that debt and cash signs match the chosen convention.

Explain the driver

Move from result to driver to implication: the ratio changed, a specific numerator or denominator caused it, and the change affects risk, return, or strategy.

Worked example

Excel: =net_income/AVERAGE(beginning_equity,ending_equity)

Manual logic: 12,000 ÷ ((28,000 + 32,000) ÷ 2)

Expected conclusion: 40.0% ROE.

Ask whether the result came from stronger profitability, asset use, or leverage. The workbook dashboard supports the driver discussion.

Source discipline

M04 uses supplied classroom data; FactSet retrieval is not required. In a later real-company application, before using a FactSet value, record the field name, company, fiscal period, units, currency, and retrieval date. Reconcile material inputs to the company filing or FactSet definition. A correct-looking formula built from mismatched periods or units is not a valid analysis.

Decision questions

  1. When might a lower current ratio reflect better management?
  2. How could a share repurchase increase ROE without improving operations?

Key takeaways

  • Use consistent definitions and periods.
  • Decompose ratios into drivers.
  • Connect every ratio to a decision implication.

Interest coverage is annual EBIT divided by annual interest expense. At 1.0x EBIT equals interest; below 1.0x, investigate cash and payment timing. A value above 1.0x is not by itself proof of safety.
